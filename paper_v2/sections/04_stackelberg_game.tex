\section{Stackelberg Consensus Game}
\label{sec:game}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Game Formulation}

We formulate multi-agent consensus as a Stackelberg game $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{F}, \Theta, \mathcal{S}, U_L, U_F)$.

\paragraph{Players.} The \emph{leader} $\mathcal{L}$ is the mechanism designer (bilevel optimizer). The \emph{followers} $\mathcal{F} = \{1, \ldots, n\}$ are the LLM agents.

\paragraph{Strategy Spaces.} The leader chooses mechanism parameters $\theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^{21}$, controlling how AEIC quality signals map to consensus weights and how collective behavioral dynamics are regulated. Each follower $i$ chooses a strategy $s_i \in \{s^{\text{ind}}, s^{\text{syc}}\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $s^{\text{ind}}$ (independent reasoning): produces high IC, EC, EQ at cost $C_{\text{ind}}$;
    \item $s^{\text{syc}}$ (sycophantic): produces lower IC/EC but potentially high EV at cost $C_{\text{syc}} < C_{\text{ind}}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Timing.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Leader commits to parameters $\theta$ (mechanism rules).
    \item Each follower observes $\theta$ and chooses strategy $s_i$.
    \item Agents produce AEIC outputs; quality signals $\tau_i$ are computed.
    \item Mechanism computes weights $w_i(\tau_i; \theta)$ via the scoring rule.
    \item Weighted voting produces the consensus decision.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Utility Functions.} The follower utility is:
\begin{equation}
    U_i(s_i; \theta) = w_i(\tau_i(s_i); \theta) \cdot R - C(s_i)
    \label{eq:follower_utility}
\end{equation}
where $R > 0$ is the reward for adoption. The leader maximizes:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\theta \in \Theta} \; \text{Accuracy}(\theta) + \lambda \cdot \text{IC}(\theta)
    \label{eq:leader_obj}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Scoring Rule}
\label{sec:scoring}

The 21 parameters $\theta$ control three aspects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bid construction} (13 parameters): How IC, EC, EQ, EV signals combine into agent ``bids'' (alignment willingness $\alpha_i$, revision cost, historical alignment $h_i$).
    \item \textbf{Stackelberg dynamics} (4 parameters): Consensus energy coefficient $\lambda_c$, budget penalty $\mu$, learning rate $\eta$, and peer pressure $\gamma$.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic regulation} (4 parameters): Sycophancy detection threshold $\tau_h$, deadlock threshold $\tau_l$, EV decay rate $\alpha$, and pressure adjustment rate $\beta$ (Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}).
\end{itemize}

Specifically, for agent $i$:
\begin{align}
    \alpha_i &= \max\!\big(\theta_{\text{floor}}, \; \text{IC}_i^{\theta_{\text{ic}}} \cdot \text{EQ}_i^{\theta_{\text{eq}}} \cdot (\theta_{\text{ev\_base}} + \theta_{\text{ev\_scale}} \cdot \text{EV}_i)\big) \\
    h_i &= \theta_{h_1} \cdot \text{IC}_i + \theta_{h_2} \cdot \text{EC}_i + \theta_{h_3} \cdot \text{EQ}_i + \theta_{h_4} \cdot \text{EV}_i
\end{align}

These bids are processed through a distributed consensus game that produces final weights $w_i(\theta)$ via iterative updates incorporating peer pressure from a graph Laplacian defined by the communication topology.

\subsection{Incentive Compatibility Metric}
\label{sec:ic_metric}

We define incentive compatibility through inter-group weight comparison:

\begin{definition}[Incentive Compatibility Metric]
For each question $q$, rank agents by quality score $\phi(\tau_i) = (\text{IC}_i + \text{EC}_i + \text{EQ}_i)/3$. Let $H_q$ = top-2 agents and $L_q$ = bottom-2 agents. Define:
\begin{equation}
    \text{IC}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\frac{1}{|H_q|}\sum_{i \in H_q} w_i(\theta) > \frac{1}{|L_q|}\sum_{j \in L_q} w_j(\theta)\right]
    \label{eq:ic_metric}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This measures the fraction of questions where higher-quality reasoning receives greater consensus weight, capturing the essence of incentive compatibility.

\subsection{Bilevel Optimization}
\label{sec:bilevel}

The Stackelberg game is solved via bilevel optimization:
\begin{align}
    \textbf{Upper (Leader):} \quad & \max_{\theta \in \Theta} \; \text{Accuracy}(\theta) + \lambda \cdot \text{IC}(\theta) \label{eq:upper} \\
    \textbf{Lower (Followers):} \quad & w_i = f(\tau_i; \theta) \quad \forall i \label{eq:lower}
\end{align}

The lower-level problem is deterministic given $\theta$ (agents' AEIC outputs are fixed offline), reducing to single-level optimization implementable via differential evolution~\cite{storn1997differential}. Per-topology $\theta^*_T$ is optimized with population size 21 (1$\times$ the number of parameters) and 30 generations, then evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation to prevent train/test leakage. We note that the Stackelberg mechanism provides formal IC guarantees (Theorem~\ref{thm:ic}), but experiments (Section~\ref{sec:exp}) show that quality-weighted voting does not outperform majority voting under homogeneous LLM agents---the bottleneck is the quality signals' limited discriminative power, not the mechanism design.

\subsection{Online Stackelberg Extension}
\label{sec:online_sg}

In multi-round topologies (debate, hierarchical), the mechanism operates \emph{online}: between rounds $t$ and $t{+}1$, it computes interim AEIC signals from round-$t$ outputs, runs the Stackelberg game to obtain current weights $w_i^{(t)}$, and injects quality feedback into agent prompts:
\begin{equation}
    \text{context}_i^{(t+1)} = \text{context}_i^{(t+1)} \cup \{(\text{IC}_i^{(t)}, \text{EC}_i^{(t)}, w_i^{(t)})\}
\end{equation}
This transforms the game from post-hoc reweighting to real-time behavioral shaping: agents observe their current quality assessment and can adjust reasoning effort accordingly. The star topology is inherently online---leaf trust scores computed via Stackelberg are injected into the hub's synthesis context.

Together with the static optimization of Section~\ref{sec:bilevel}, this establishes a two-layer Stackelberg evolution: static optimization discovers \emph{what} behaviors to suppress (e.g., near-zero EV weights on information-sharing topologies), while online feedback communicates \emph{how} each agent is currently performing. However, the online extension injects the same quality feedback regardless of the collective behavioral pattern---it cannot distinguish sycophantic herding from productive convergence. Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} introduces a third layer that addresses this limitation.

\subsection{Dynamic Sycophancy Regulation}
\label{sec:dynamic}

Online Stackelberg feedback (Section~\ref{sec:online_sg}) injects quality signals between rounds but applies a uniform intervention regardless of collective behavior. In practice, multi-round deliberation exhibits distinct pathological patterns: \emph{sycophantic convergence}, where agents rapidly abandon independent positions to follow the emerging majority, and \emph{deadlock}, where agents rigidly maintain initial positions without engaging with opposing evidence. We introduce a dynamic regulation layer that monitors collective stance dynamics and applies differentiated interventions.

\paragraph{Behavioral Metrics.}
Between rounds $t{-}1$ and $t$, we compute three metrics from agent conclusions $c_i^{(t)}$ (reliably extracted from the AEIC Conclusion~field):
\begin{align}
    \text{SCR}^{(t)} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{1}\!\left[c_i^{(t)} \neq c_i^{(t-1)}\right] \label{eq:scr} \\
    \text{MR}^{(t)} &= \max\!\left(\frac{|\{i : c_i^{(t)} = \text{yes}\}|}{n},\; \frac{|\{i : c_i^{(t)} = \text{no}\}|}{n}\right) \label{eq:mr} \\
    \text{CS}^{(t)} &= \left|\text{MR}^{(t)} - \text{MR}^{(t-1)}\right| \label{eq:cs}
\end{align}
where SCR (Stance Change Rate) measures the fraction of agents that changed their conclusion, MR (Majority Ratio) measures the dominance of the leading position, and CS (Convergence Speed) captures how rapidly the majority is growing.

\paragraph{State Classification.}
The system classifies each inter-round transition into one of three states:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sycophantic} ($\text{SCR} \geq \tau_h$): A large fraction of agents changed their stance, indicating potential herding behavior where agents abandon independent reasoning to follow the emerging majority.
    \item \textbf{Deadlock} ($\text{SCR} \leq \tau_l \wedge \text{MR} < 0.8$): Few agents changed stance and no clear majority exists, indicating entrenched positions without productive evidence exchange.
    \item \textbf{Healthy} (otherwise): Moderate stance evolution with either gradual convergence or stable disagreement---no intervention needed.
\end{itemize}
The thresholds $\tau_h$, $\tau_l$ and the response parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$ (below) are included in the learnable parameter vector $\theta$, extending the base 17 parameters to 21 dimensions. The bilevel optimization (Section~\ref{sec:bilevel}) jointly discovers optimal detection thresholds and response intensities alongside the base Stackelberg parameters.

\paragraph{Adaptive Response.}
Upon detecting a non-healthy state, the mechanism responds through two channels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parameter adjustment}: For sycophantic states, the evidence consensus weight is decayed: $\theta_{\text{ev\_scale}} \leftarrow \theta_{\text{ev\_scale}} \cdot (1 - \alpha)$, suppressing the very signal that rewards conformity. Peer pressure and consensus energy are similarly reduced: $\gamma \leftarrow \gamma \cdot (1 - \beta)$, $\lambda_c \leftarrow \lambda_c \cdot (1 - \beta)$. For deadlock, these parameters are \emph{increased} to encourage engagement. Both $\alpha \in [0.1, 0.9]$ and $\beta \in [0.1, 0.6]$ are optimized by the leader.
    \item \textbf{Differentiated prompts}: The next-round context is augmented with state-specific instructions: anti-sycophancy prompts emphasize independent reasoning over majority-following; exchange prompts encourage engaging with opposing evidence; neutral prompts provide standard deliberation guidance.
\end{enumerate}

This completes a three-layer Stackelberg evolution: \emph{static} optimization discovers which behaviors to suppress (Section~\ref{sec:bilevel}), \emph{online} feedback communicates individual quality assessments (Section~\ref{sec:online_sg}), and \emph{dynamic} regulation detects and corrects collective behavioral pathologies in real time. The key insight is that the Stackelberg game's value lies in \emph{collective behavior regulation}---shaping how the group deliberates---rather than in quality-based selection of individual contributions.
