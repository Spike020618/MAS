\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Why Stackelberg over Nash?}
Yi et al.~\cite{yi2025debate} model multi-agent debate as a simultaneous-move Bayesian game. Our Stackelberg formulation is more natural for mechanism design: the aggregation rule is \emph{committed to} before agents act, enabling the leader to shape follower behavior through credible commitment---a property absent in Nash equilibrium where all players move simultaneously.

\paragraph{Quality Signals vs.\ Correctness.}
Our most significant finding is that AEIC quality signals measure reasoning process quality, not answer correctness. McNemar tests confirm that AEIC+SG weighted voting is significantly \emph{worse} than majority voting on three topologies (parallel $p{=}0.010$, sequential $p{=}0.004$, debate $p{=}0.003$) and only significantly better on hierarchical ($p{=}0.017$). A coherent, well-evidenced wrong answer scores as highly as a correct one. This implies that quality-based weighting cannot substitute for diversity-based aggregation when agents share the same LLM backend and similar knowledge biases.

\paragraph{Improving Signal Fidelity.}
The embedding-based similarity (MiniLM) used for IC and EC measures topical relevance rather than logical entailment---a coherent wrong answer about topic $X$ is semantically close to a correct answer about the same topic. Replacing cosine similarity with a Natural Language Inference (NLI) model (\eg, DeBERTa-v3~NLI) could improve signal fidelity by distinguishing logical support from mere semantic overlap. Preliminary experiments with a multilingual NLI model (mDeBERTa-v3-xnli) on 50 MMLU-Hard questions reveal the challenge: self-consistency NLI (does $I_i$ entail $C_i$?) achieves only AUC${}=0.55$ for predicting agent correctness, marginally above cosine similarity (AUC${}=0.47$). A stronger signal emerges from cross-agent NLI calibration---checking whether each agent's reasoning entails the majority conclusion (AUC${}=0.78$)---but this reduces to majority agreement (AUC${}=0.78$ for simple stance matching), providing no incremental information. The fundamental limitation is that \emph{self-consistency cannot distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning} when agents share the same LLM and produce similarly coherent outputs regardless of correctness. Similarly, EV currently measures evidence consensus via cosine similarity, which becomes an echo-chamber metric when agents share the same LLM backend. Breaking this barrier likely requires heterogeneous agent pools where diverse knowledge bases create meaningful quality variation. Notably, the router's success (87.6\%) does not stem from quality signals overcoming this limitation at a meta-level: feature ablation shows that voting-pattern features---majority ratio and yes/no distribution extracted from AEIC-structured conclusions---drive routing accuracy ($+2.1$pp), while quality signal groups contribute marginally or negatively. The structured decomposition itself, not the quality measurement, is what enables effective routing.

\paragraph{AEIC as Structured Reasoning Protocol.}
The structured output ablation (Table~\ref{tab:structured}) provides the most direct evidence for AEIC's value: majority voting from the explicit Conclusion~field achieves 76.9\% accuracy versus 48.9\% from the unstructured Inference field (+28.0pp), driven by the C-field's low neutral rate (12.7\%) versus the I-field's 66.4\%. Router feature ablation further confirms this: retraining with voting-only features yields 86.3\% (vs.\ 86.9\% full), while quality-only features yield 86.1\%---the two feature groups are largely redundant, and both independently provide ${\sim}$5.5pp over question-only features (80.7\%). The appropriate framing for AEIC in homogeneous-agent systems is thus a \emph{structured reasoning protocol} that standardizes output format for reliable aggregation and enables the voting-pattern features that drive adaptive routing, rather than a quality measurement framework.

\paragraph{Anti-Sycophancy from First Principles.}
Optimal mechanisms learn near-zero EV weights on star and debate topologies, providing a principled anti-sycophancy mechanism. On information-sharing topologies, high EV reflects social influence rather than independent verification. This emerges from accuracy optimization without explicit constraints, complementing heuristic approaches~\cite{pitre2025consensagent} with a game-theoretic foundation.

\paragraph{Oracle Gap Analysis.}
The 5.3pp gap between routing (87.6\%) and oracle (92.9\%) arises from questions where no topology produces correct consensus (7.0\%) and routing errors where the selected topology fails despite a correct alternative existing. Analyzing topology agreement: on 62.1\% of questions, all five topologies agree (55.1\% all correct, 7.0\% all wrong); routing adds no value here. The router's impact comes from the 9.0\% of ``hard'' questions where only 1--2 topologies succeed. Our router closes 46.5\% of the gap between the best fixed topology (83.1\%) and the oracle. The remaining gap could be reduced by richer topology spaces, heterogeneous agent pools, or improved routing features that better distinguish question difficulty patterns.

\paragraph{Comparison with RL-Based Adaptive Topology.}
RUMAD~\cite{wang2026rumad} uses reinforcement learning to adaptively control debate rounds and agent participation within a single topology type. Our approach differs in three ways: (1)~we select among five fundamentally different communication structures (parallel, sequential, star, debate, hierarchical), not just debate variants; (2)~our routing uses interpretable voting-pattern features extracted from AEIC-structured outputs with game-theoretic grounding, while RUMAD uses opaque RL policies; (3)~our Stackelberg mechanism provides formal incentive compatibility guarantees (Theorem~\ref{thm:ic}). The approaches are complementary---within-topology adaptation (RUMAD) and cross-topology selection (ours) could be combined.

\paragraph{Online Stackelberg Game.}
Our online extension injects quality feedback between rounds, transforming Stackelberg from post-hoc reweighting to real-time incentive shaping. The experimental results (Table~\ref{tab:online}) reveal that effectiveness depends critically on topology--task interaction: debate benefits on knowledge-intensive tasks (+6.7pp on MMLU-Hard at $n{=}30$), while hierarchical shows no improvement ($-4.4$pp). The mixed results---particularly the degradation on TruthfulQA and hierarchical---directly motivate the dynamic regulation mechanism.

\paragraph{From Quality Selection to Behavioral Regulation.}
The three-layer Stackelberg evolution reveals a progression in what the mechanism learns to control. \emph{Static} optimization discovers \emph{which} behaviors to suppress---EV weights converge to near-zero on debate and star topologies, providing principled anti-sycophancy without explicit constraints. \emph{Online} feedback communicates \emph{how each agent is performing}---injecting quality assessments between rounds so agents can self-correct. \emph{Dynamic} regulation detects \emph{what the collective is doing}---monitoring stance change rates and majority ratios to distinguish productive convergence from sycophantic herding or deadlock, then applying targeted interventions. This progression reflects a shift from individual quality selection (which fails when agents share the same LLM and produce similarly coherent outputs) to collective behavior regulation (which operates on observable group dynamics regardless of individual signal quality). At $n{=}50$, dynamic regulation achieves a +8pp improvement over baseline on MMLU-Hard (94.0\% vs.\ 86.0\%, Table~\ref{tab:dynamic}), with the effect concentrated on knowledge-intensive questions requiring genuine iterative deliberation. On StrategyQA and TruthfulQA, dynamic regulation maintains baseline accuracy despite static feedback's degradation, confirming that selective intervention avoids the noise introduced by uniform quality exposure. Grid-search analysis over the detection thresholds further indicates that the current settings ($\tau_h{=}0.6$, $\tau_l{=}0.2$) are conservative: $\tau_h{=}0.45$, $\tau_l{=}0.05$ would produce stronger accuracy separation between intervened and non-intervened transitions, suggesting that joint bilevel optimization over all 21 parameters---including detection thresholds---can unlock further gains through task-specific tuning. Notably, per-dataset grid search reveals that optimal thresholds differ across task types: MMLU-Hard benefits from aggressive detection ($\tau_h{=}0.35$) given strong SCR--correctness separation (correct SCR mean 0.19 vs.\ wrong 0.30), while StrategyQA achieves clear pathological--healthy separation at $\tau_h{=}0.45$ (sycophantic accuracy 36\% vs.\ healthy 80\%), and TruthfulQA shows weak SCR discrimination (correct 0.24 vs.\ wrong 0.26), explaining why dynamic regulation adds little on factual tasks. This task-dependence of optimal thresholds mirrors the broader finding that Stackelberg mechanisms benefit from per-topology optimization (Section~\ref{sec:exp}). A finer-grained analysis of MMLU-Hard reveals that the deadlock intervention, while valuable overall, occasionally backfires on difficult questions where a correct minority position needs extended deliberation time to persuade others rather than forced exchange---questions harmed by dynamic regulation averaged 1.3 interventions versus 1.0 for those helped. Unlike RL-based adaptive approaches~\cite{wang2026rumad}, our dynamic regulation is fully interpretable---three metrics, three states, and two intervention channels---and requires no additional training, making such failure-mode diagnosis straightforward.

\paragraph{Convergence Dynamics and Intervention Channels.}
Round-by-round majority ratio (MR) trajectories reveal distinct convergence patterns across datasets. On MMLU-Hard, MR rises from 0.76 (Round~1) to 0.82 (Round~3) under dynamic regulation, consistent with productive iterative refinement. On StrategyQA, MR \emph{declines} from 0.81 to 0.75---agents become less aligned over rounds, suggesting that debate structure degrades rather than improves consensus on commonsense questions. The MR trajectory strongly predicts outcomes: correct answers maintain high MR throughout (0.81$\to$0.83$\to$0.80), while incorrect answers show a characteristic dip at Round~2 (MR 0.67) where the group briefly converges toward the wrong answer before partially recovering. This ``wrong valley'' pattern accounts for most of the 6\% net dynamic benefit on MMLU-Hard, where timely anti-sycophancy interventions at Round~2 transitions prevent incorrect convergence.

Channel-level analysis of the 63 interventions across 150 dynamic-condition questions reveals that the two intervention channels---parameter adjustment and prompt templates---always co-occur: no transition receives prompt modification without parameter changes or vice versa. The anti-sycophancy template paired with EV suppression achieves 83.3\% question-level accuracy on MMLU-Hard but only 36.4\% on StrategyQA, confirming that prompt-level interventions are task-sensitive. The deadlock intervention (exchange encouragement + increased peer pressure) achieves 82.4\% on MMLU-Hard versus 50.0\% on StrategyQA. This systematic gap suggests that StrategyQA's failure mode is not correctable by the current intervention types---the commonsense reasoning process may require fundamentally different regulatory strategies than the knowledge-intensive reasoning where dynamic regulation excels.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(1)~The router requires running all five topologies for feature extraction (5$\times$ cost); online deployment needs topology prediction from question features alone.
(2)~All agents use the same LLM (DeepSeek-V3); heterogeneous pools may behave differently.
(3)~Online Stackelberg feedback shows mixed results: beneficial on MMLU-Hard debate but degrading on TruthfulQA and hierarchical, suggesting the need for task-adaptive feedback policies.
(4)~The dynamic regulation evaluation uses $n{=}50$ per dataset; the $+8$pp MMLU-Hard improvement has a 95\% bootstrap CI of $[-4, +20]$pp, requiring larger-scale validation for firm statistical significance.
(5)~Question sets are not fully shared across topologies (only 633 of 900 questions overlap across all five), limiting the router evaluation set.
